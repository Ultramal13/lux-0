LUX-0 · How it works
====================

What it is
----------
LUX-0 is an Android app that uses the phone's camera as a light sensor.
It does not take pictures. It measures the color temperature (Kelvin) and
the green/magenta tint of the light, then translates the result into the
values you set by hand on external cameras (for example a Canon R5 or an
Insta360 Luna Ultra).


Technology
----------
- Platform: native Android (Android 12 or later).
- Language: Kotlin.
- UI:       Jetpack Compose, with an original "handheld device" design.
- Camera:   Camera2, Android's low-level camera API. It is used instead of
            CameraX because the app needs control over the camera's color
            processing.
- Build:    Gradle 9 + Android Gradle Plugin 9, JDK 21, Android SDK 37.
- Data:     everything stays on the phone (a JSON file plus settings). No
            cloud, no accounts, no internet.
- Tests:    JUnit for the math. The UI is also checked on the Android
            emulator in demo mode.
- Libraries: only official Android/Google ones (AndroidX, Compose). No
            third-party libraries or external services.


How it measures
---------------
1. Small image. The camera delivers a small image (640x480, YUV) in the same
   lightweight mode the camera app uses for its viewfinder, at a low frame
   rate.

2. No phone "makeup". The color processing the phone normally applies is
   turned off: auto white balance off, an identity color matrix with unit
   gains, and a linear tone curve. The numbers are what the sensor sees, not
   what the phone thinks things should look like.

3. Measuring area. The app averages the color of a center circle. You point
   it at a white sheet or gray card, or you put a white diffuser over the lens
   and point it at the light. The app warns if the area is clipped, too dark,
   or not neutral.

4. From sensor color to real color. Every sensor ships with factory color
   calibration matrices, which Android exposes (the same ones Adobe's DNG
   format uses). They convert the sensor RGB to the standard CIE 1931 color
   space (xy coordinates), interpolating between the two matrices by
   temperature, as DNG does.

5. Kelvin and tint. From xy the app computes:
   - the correlated color temperature (CCT): the closest point on the
     black-body curve (Planckian locus), using the Krystek (1985)
     approximation in CIE 1960 uv
   - the Duv: the distance to that curve. Positive means green, negative
     means magenta.

6. Translation to each camera. A small database stores each model's Kelvin
   range, step size, and tint system (for example Canon's +/-9 G/M WB
   Shift). The app rounds to those steps and gives a suggested value plus a
   range.

7. Optional calibration. You compare against a known reference: shoot a
   white sheet in RAW with the R5, read the Kelvin with the white balance
   eyedropper in Lightroom or DPP, and enter it in the app. The app stores
   the correction (in mired).


Other tools (stage 2)
---------------------
- Exposure: takes the time, ISO, and aperture the phone picks to expose the
  circle (with -1 EV compensation) and computes EV100. It corrects for what you
  aim at (18% gray or 90% white). With each camera's ISO, shutter, ND, and EV,
  it suggests an aperture. This is reflected metering: about +/-1/3-1/2 stop
  after calibration.
- Lux: the phone's front light sensor (the auto-brightness one).
- Flicker: the camera switches to 1/2000 s (only on that screen, at 15 fps
  max). A pulsing light leaves horizontal bands, because each sensor row is
  read a little later than the one before. A least-squares fit of the row
  profile gives the frequency (100 Hz on 50 Hz mains, 120 Hz on 60 Hz) and its
  depth. A shutter is safe if it lasts a whole number of cycles.
- Compare: two frozen readings. The mired difference maps to CTO/CTB gels
  (published values), and the Duv difference maps to approximate
  Plus/Minus Green.
- Map: the image is split into 8x6 cells, each one measured with the same
  method, and colored relative to the reference.


Battery and heat care
---------------------
- One frame per second is analyzed (configurable). Flicker mode analyzes at
  most one per second.
- If Android reports the phone is warming up, the app measures more slowly.
  At a moderate level it turns the camera off, and resumes only after 60 s of
  calm.
- Automatic pause after 90 s without touching the screen. "Hold" freezes the
  reading and turns the camera off. In lux, sessions, and settings the camera
  is off.
- An early version captured full-resolution RAW every second and heated the
  phone. It was replaced by the lightweight method described above. Test of
  the current version on an S26 Ultra: +1.9 C over one minute of use, with no
  thermal warning.


Security and privacy
--------------------
- No internet, no accounts, no ads, no analytics.
- Data stays on the phone. It is excluded from cloud backup and from device
  transfer.
- The CSV leaves the phone only when the user exports it, and formulas are
  neutralized for Excel and Sheets.
- The app never touches the torch or system settings. Manual camera values are
  always clamped to the ranges the device reports.


Expected accuracy (estimated, being validated in the beta)
----------------------------------------------------------
- White sheet or diffuser, calibrated:   +/-150-300 K.
- "Scene" mode (no reference):           +/-500 K or more.
- Exposure:                              +/-1/3-1/2 stop.
- Canon tint is approximate, because Canon does not publish the exact
  equivalence between its G/M steps and Duv.


Is any of this secret?
----------------------
No. Everything is based on public standards:
- CIE colorimetry (1931 and 1960)
- the Planckian locus and the Krystek approximation
- the DNG color model
- Google's documented Camera2 API

What is specific to LUX-0 is how the pieces fit together.

Status: public beta v0.2.0, with stages 1 and 2 complete.
